# H2.1 mixture-model recovery-grid FAIL — what happened, what it means

## The big picture

The Roman inscription corpus you're working with has a fundamental problem that's invisible to a casual reader of the data: when an inscription is hard to date, editors fall back on conventions. They round to centuries, anchor to emperors' reigns, or use canonical templates like "AD 100–200" or "1st century AD". When you draw a summed-probability-of-activity plot from the corpus — counting how much "inscription activity" sits on each year — those conventions create spikes and plateaus that look like real historical patterns but are actually editorial fingerprints.

The H2.1 mixture model is designed to **separate those two contributions**. It says: what we see is a weighted blend of two things — a *convention* component (the editorial template) and a *genuine* component (whatever real ancient activity gives rise to the inscriptions). The single most important quantity it estimates is **α** (alpha), the fraction of what we observe that's editorial artefact. If α is high (say, 0.95), almost all of what we see is editorial — the corpus is mostly a record of how dating conventions evolved, with little signal about ancient behaviour. If α is low (say, 0.05), only a small fraction is editorial — most of what we see really is the ancient activity pattern.

α matters because every substantive question — was there more inscribing activity under Hadrian than Trajan? did inscription production track the urbanisation curve? — depends on knowing how much of the apparent pattern is real signal. Without α, you can't disentangle the two.

## What we did: building a model and testing whether it works

Before applying the model to the real LIRE corpus (180,609 inscriptions filtered to 50 BC – AD 350), we needed to know whether it works. The test we use is called a **recovery simulation**, and the logic is simple:

1. Make synthetic data where we **already know the answer**: we set α to (say) 0.50 and pick a particular shape for the genuine component (say, a smooth decline over the AD 50–350 envelope), then generate fake inscription counts under that ground truth.
2. Fit the model to the synthetic data, pretending we don't know α or the shape.
3. Check: did the model recover α and the shape correctly?

If yes, we can trust it on the real data. If no, we need to understand why before we trust anything it tells us about Rome.

The full recovery test was a **450-cell grid**: every combination of 5 α values (0.05, 0.30, 0.50, 0.70, 0.95) × 6 shapes (bimodal, flat, regnal-clustered, rise-and-fall, smooth-decline, smooth-growth) × 5 tier-weight templates × 3 corpus sizes. For each cell we generated 100 different synthetic datasets and fit the model to each, so we could measure not just the average behaviour but the variability. That's 45,000 separate Bayesian fits — about 30 hours of sapphire compute total.

Two pass/fail criteria, set in advance in the preregistration:

- **α-coverage**: the model's 95 % credible interval for α should contain the true α at least 90 % of the time.
- **Shape recovery**: a quality metric (Pearson r) comparing the recovered curve to the truth should be ≥ 0.95.

Both criteria had to be met by ≥ 90 % of cells for the model to pass.

## What the failure looked like

Only **40.9 %** of cells passed both criteria. That's a hard FAIL. But "FAIL" doesn't mean the model is worthless — it means the model has some specific failure modes, and we need to know what they are before we can decide what to do.

The diagnostic investigation found two distinct stories tangled up in that single FAIL number, and the follow-up investigations (F0/F1/F3) cleanly separated them.

## Story 1: a measurement artefact, not a model failure

Some of the FAIL was the recovery grid scoring itself badly rather than the model misbehaving. The `flat_baseline` shape — where the "true" generative pattern is just uniform across the envelope — automatically failed the shape-recovery criterion because **Pearson r is mathematically undefined when one of the things you're correlating has zero variance**. A constant has zero variance. So the criterion returned `NaN` (not-a-number), which was treated as a fail.

When we looked at what the model actually recovered in those `flat_baseline` cells, it was *almost perfectly flat* (variance around 10⁻⁹; max deviation around 10⁻⁴ in absolute terms — invisible noise). The model was doing exactly the right thing; we'd just chosen a measuring stick that breaks on flat surfaces.

The fix here is mechanical: switch the binding shape metric from Pearson r to **Wasserstein-1** ("earth-mover distance"), which measures how much probability mass has to move to transform one curve into another. It's well-defined on flat curves — it just returns small numbers when the recovery is close to flat. From F0b, we know that a W-1 threshold of 18.6 years would reproduce the current Pearson-r-≥-0.95 selectivity on non-flat cells, and W-1 ≤ 5 years would be markedly stricter. Martin will help pick the threshold.

## Story 2: a real model bias that we can't easily fix

The harder story is about α. Here's what F0a (the systematics analysis across all 450 cells) revealed:

| true α | model's mean estimate | bias |
|--:|--:|--:|
| 0.05 | 0.120 | **+0.070** (overestimates) |
| 0.30 | 0.290 | −0.010 |
| 0.50 | 0.456 | −0.044 |
| 0.70 | 0.640 | −0.060 |
| 0.95 | 0.885 | −0.065 |

The model is **shrinking α toward the middle of its range**. When the truth is low, the estimate is too high. When the truth is high, the estimate is too low. The maximum bias is about 0.07 — small in absolute terms, but systematic and consistent across shapes and corpus sizes.

The underlying cause is something Bayesian statisticians call a **likelihood ridge** between α and the shape of the genuine component. Here's the intuition: the model can fit the observed data approximately equally well with two very different stories. Story A: "high α + a structured genuine pattern that mostly cancels out the convention spikes." Story B: "lower α + a smoother genuine pattern that doesn't need to do as much work." The observed counts can't tell these apart — they're equally likely under either story. Faced with that ambiguity, the model splits the difference, ending up biased toward middle-α with moderately-smooth-shape.

The original investigation's mental model was that this was a problem **only at α = 0.95** — that the model breaks down at the extreme. F0 corrected that: the bias starts at α = 0.30 and saturates by α = 0.70. **The whole range is affected**, not just the high end. What collapses at α = 0.95 specifically is the shape-recovery quality (Pearson r drops from 78 % pass to 22 %), not the α-bias itself.

## What fixes we tried, and what happened

There were two cheap candidate fixes the literature pointed to. Both turned out to be wrong-but-informative.

### F1 — sharper α prior

A "prior" in Bayesian statistics is the model's initial belief about a parameter, before seeing data. The H2.1 model uses a `Beta(2, 2)` prior on α — a symmetric, mildly-concentrated belief centred on 0.5. We thought maybe this prior was *pulling* the estimate toward 0.5, and that loosening it to a flat prior (`Uniform(0, 1)`, equivalent to `Beta(1, 1)`) would let the data speak more.

We re-ran the same three α = 0.95 cells × three effort levels (9 fits) with the loosened prior. **Result: the estimate moved by only +0.025 on average** (range +0.004 to +0.037). The threshold we'd pre-set for "prior pull is a substantial contributor" was +0.05; we missed it by a lot. **The prior is not the problem.** The data itself is what's pulling the estimate toward the middle — the likelihood ridge is doing nearly all the work.

### F3 — non-centred GRW reparameterisation

This one's more technical but the intuition is approachable. The genuine component of the mixture is constructed via a **Gaussian random walk** on log-density values — basically, a smoothness constraint that says "the genuine pattern shouldn't jump wildly from one year to the next". The standard textbook way to code this is what's called a "centred parameterisation". It's mathematically clean but creates a sampler geometry that's hard for the Bayesian inference engine to navigate efficiently — like asking someone to walk through a long narrow funnel.

The "non-centred" reparameterisation is a mathematical re-write that gives the *same prior* (mathematically equivalent) but a different *geometry* (a flat plain instead of a funnel). It's the standard recommended cure for sampler problems in this class of model. We thought maybe the sampler was getting stuck in a corner of the funnel and not exploring the right answer.

We re-fit the three α = 0.95 cells with the non-centred version. **Result: the α estimate moved by +0.001 on average** (range −0.003 to +0.005). Essentially zero change. **The sampler isn't the problem either** — the chain was already finding the right posterior; that posterior is just biased.

There was a **silver lining** in F3 though. While the parameterisation didn't change the answer, it changed the *quality* of the sampling dramatically. The effective sample size — a measure of how independent the posterior draws are — went up by 45-50×. The R-hat convergence diagnostic went from 1.04 (mediocre) to 1.0008 (excellent). Divergences (numerical pathologies in the sampler) went from a few to zero. **Adopting the non-centred parameterisation as the default is a free win on computational efficiency** even though it doesn't fix the bias. We'll roll it into the production code in Phase 2.

### What both negative results jointly tell us

Two cheap fixes ruled out. The bias isn't from the prior (too tight a belief about α), and it isn't from the sampler (struggling with funnel geometry). What's left is **structural unidentifiability**: the model is asking the data to disentangle α from shape complexity, and the data can't fully do it. The information needed to nail down α isn't in the data at all — it would have to come from a different prior, a different model structure, or auxiliary information.

That's the question Martin's input is for. The remaining candidate moves are bigger interventions — e.g., a **more rigid shape prior** that breaks the ridge by forcing the genuine component to be smoother; an **ordered-mixture parameterisation** that constrains the relative complexity of the two components; or a **Dirichlet-process residual** that puts a structural cap on how much the genuine component can curve. Any of these is a larger change, and we want Martin's view on which is the right one for an applied-econometrician audience.

## What this means for the inscriptions data, in practical terms

A few translations into the real research:

**The model is usable, but with a known caveat.** When we eventually run it on the real LIRE corpus, whatever α it reports will be **shrunk toward 0.5** relative to the truth. If the true α for LIRE is around 0.30 (a plausible mid-range guess for an editorially-curated corpus), the model will report something like 0.29 — close enough to truth that conclusions are safe. If the true α is around 0.70, the model will report around 0.64 — a substantive underestimate, and a real concern. Until we know roughly where on the α axis the corpus sits, we can't say how worried to be.

**The implications cascade into the historical questions.** Take the urbanisation question (Hanson scaling, H3a in the prereg). If the model under-reports α, it implies the model is *over*-attributing observed patterns to ancient activity (because what's left for the "genuine" component to do is larger than truth). That could make Hanson scaling β look slightly steeper than reality — i.e., the relationship between population and inscription rate would appear stronger. Whether that's a 1 % concern or a 10 % concern depends on calibrating α-bias against the real corpus, which is on the post-Martin work tranche.

**The flat-shape and high-α regimes are partially-known-broken.** For research questions where we expect α to be very high (corpora dominated by templated dedications, formulaic epitaphs), the model is currently unreliable. For research questions where we expect the genuine pattern to be approximately flat (e.g., a steady-rate baseline), the binding criterion needs to switch from Pearson r to W-1 before we can score recoveries fairly.

**The regnal-cluster shape has a separate, weirder bias.** Shapes that put genuine mass on specific reign-windows (Hadrian, Trajan) show a *bidirectional* bias — overestimating α at low truth, underestimating at high truth — which suggests the model is re-attributing reign-specific signal between the two components depending on which one ends up being "structurally simpler" at the fitted values. This might matter for substantive analyses targeting specific imperial reigns. F0 flagged it; Q2 in the Martin pack asks him whether there's a diagnostic that pre-flags this regime in real-data work.

**Phase 2 is not blocked.** We can do quite a lot before Martin's reply. Adopting non-centred GRW in the production code is a clean immediate win. Generating the W-1 distribution figures for co-author review is non-blocking. Multi-replicate verification of the bias direction (10 replicates × hardest × 3 cells, about an hour of compute) would strengthen the empirical record. What we *can't* do without Martin: re-run the full 450-cell grid under any structural fix (~30 h compute, only worth it once we've picked the right fix); finalise the OSF amendment; revise the prereg-binding-criteria wording.

---

The mixture model has a real, named flaw, but it's a flaw we now understand mechanistically — a likelihood ridge between α and shape complexity that pulls α toward the middle of its range. The Pearson-r-on-flat-shapes problem is a measurement artefact and is cheaply fixed. The sampling efficiency problem is solved gratis by adopting non-centred GRW. What's not yet solved is the structural-identifiability question, and that's a worthwhile thing to put 90 minutes of an applied econometrician's time into tomorrow.
