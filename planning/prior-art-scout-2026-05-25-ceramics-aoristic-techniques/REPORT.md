# Prior-art report: Techniques from the ceramics-aoristic-dating community adoptable for the LIRE mixture model

## Executive summary

The ceramics-aoristic community does have implementable techniques beyond what the LIRE model already incorporates, but the overlap is substantial and the genuinely novel additions number roughly six to eight. The ceramicists have solved some problems more crisply than the inscription literature: in particular, the **dual-dating sensitivity design** (production vs. consumption chronology), the **per-item phase-confidence weight** (Bevan/Conolly 2013), and the **sampling-intensity normalisation via CPUE** (Orton/Archseries 2017) are all actionable and none appear in the current LIRE toolkit. Conversely, the ceramicists largely do not use mixture-model decomposition or explicit editorial-template separation — they treat heterogeneous chronological conventions as a sensitivity problem rather than a structural model component. This means the LIRE approach is genuinely novel in its mixture architecture, and the ceramic literature provides useful precedent for the surrounding diagnostic and validation machinery rather than a competing decomposition method. The honest answer is: **adopt 3-4 techniques directly (low cost, high value), adapt 2-3 for the paper's framing, and build on scratch the parts that are genuinely novel to the inscription context**.

| 1 | Dual-dating sensitivity test (production vs. consumption chronology) | Franconi et al. 2023 (10.1371/journal.pone.0279382), §Methods; Komar et al. 2025 (10.1007/s10816-024-09686-1) | Run the entire aoristic analysis twice: once using artefact-type production date ranges (when the form was made) and once using site occupation / deposition dates (when the object was in use). Compare the two outputs as a sensitivity check; divergence reveals how much the typological bracketing versus the depositional context drives the resulting temporal curve. | Our LIRE family classifier already codes interval structure (F1/F3/F2_Other/Tight), but we have not systematically compared what happens when we re-date using a "tighter" vs. "looser" conventional bracket assumption. A controlled dual-run — once using the empirical p_conv basis (F1+F3 intervals as-recorded) and once using only the Tight/F2_Other calibration-cohort intervals as if they were the ground truth — would quantify how much of our recovered p_gen shape is bracket-sensitive. This is exactly the logic Komar et al. use to check whether their amphora consumption curves are artefact of the typological dates vs. the site dates. | <1 day (diagnostic only; does not require model restructure, just running two variants of the data pipeline) | HIGH |
| 2 | Per-item phase-confidence weight | Bevan, Conolly et al. 2013 (10.1111/j.1475-4754.2012.00674.x), §Methods / Table 2 | Ceramicists at Antikythera asked specialists to assign a percentage confidence that each sherd belonged to each phase (e.g., "70% Hellenistic, 30% Late Roman") rather than treating phase membership as binary. The aoristic weight for sherd *i* in bin *t* is then `w_it = confidence_i × (1/Δτ_i)` rather than the default `1/Δτ_i`. This separates within-interval uncertainty (how wide the bracket is) from membership uncertainty (how confident we are the item belongs to this bracket at all). | Our model currently treats every inscription in a family as having p=1 membership in its stated `[not_before, not_after]` interval. But datation confidence varies substantially: a Tight (≤4 year) interval is qualitatively more certain than a F1 round-number slab. We could assign a per-record confidence weight derived from interval length (short = high confidence, long = low confidence) or from the family classifier (Tight → w=1.0, F1 century slab → w=0.7, F3 periodic → w=0.8) and propagate this into the aoristic spreading before model fitting. This would sharpen the calibration-cohort signal and make the p_gen prior estimate more reliable. | 1-3 days (requires adding a confidence column to the family classifier output and modifying the aoristic pre-processing step; model itself unchanged) | HIGH |
| 3 | CPUE-style sampling-intensity denominator | Orton, Morris & Pipe 2017 (10.5334/oq.29), §Methods / Fig. 5C; archSeries R package (github.com/davidcorton/archSeries) | Instead of reporting a raw frequency SPA, divide the observed count by a denominator SPA constructed from a null expectation — for Orton et al., sediment volume excavated per period. The ratio (observed / expected-under-uniform-deposition) removes artefacts of differential excavation intensity. The null is constructed by simulating a dummy assemblage sampled with probability proportional to the volume-SPA, then comparing observed and simulated. | Our inscription corpus has a known sampling-intensity confound: provinces with more inscriptions simply have more epigraphic activity, but also more modern excavation and publication effort. We do not currently correct for the intensity of epigraphic fieldwork per period. A CPUE adaptation would use the number of LIRE provenanced sites active per 5-year bin as the denominator SPA, producing an inscription-rate-per-active-site curve rather than a raw frequency curve. This separates genuine temporal intensity from the archaeological/epigraphic attention signal. | 1-3 days (requires constructing the site-active-count time series from LIRE provenance data; the ratio is straightforward once that denominator is in hand) | MEDIUM (the denominator may itself be confounded, but the method is transparent and peer-reviewed) |
| 4 | Interval-length bias correction (duration reweighting) | Bevan & Crema 2021 (10.1098/rstb.2019.0726), §2 / §4; discussed in Crema 2022 (10.1007/s10816-022-09559-5) | Bevan & Crema show that simple aoristic summation over-represents artefacts with long date ranges relative to those with short ranges, producing spurious peaks at the edges of long intervals. The correction they propose is to weight each observation by the inverse of its interval length, i.e., `w_i = 1/Δτ_i × (1/mean(1/Δτ))`, normalised so the total weight is preserved. This is the "modifiable reporting unit" fix for uneven event durations. | Our p_gen recovery already benefits indirectly from the empirical-Bayes calibration-cohort (which excludes the longest F1 slabs), but we have not applied explicit duration reweighting to the full corpus SPA before fitting. Since F1 century-slabs (100-year intervals) and Tight datings (≤4-year intervals) coexist in the SPA, there is a structural duration-heterogeneity bias in the observed SPA. Applying this correction to the pre-model SPA would give a sharper empirical target for the model to fit. Note: this is distinct from what the mixture model does — the model accounts for the slab *template* shape but not for the within-family length heterogeneity. | <1 day (a single weighting step in data preprocessing; well-documented in the literature) | HIGH |
| 5 | Quantification-method stratification for sensitivity | Franconi et al. 2023 (10.1371/journal.pone.0279382), §Data / §Results | When combining data from heterogeneous sources that use different quantification methods (MNI vs. MAX vs. sherd count), Franconi et al. analyse each quantification stratum separately and present the three resulting curves side-by-side rather than forcing a single normalised composite. Divergence between strata is itself an interpretive signal about the effect of reporting convention. | LIRE inscriptions come from multiple corpora with heterogeneous dating conventions: CIL vs. AE vs. ILS vs. local corpora use different typological and regional conventions that map imperfectly onto our family classifier. Running the full pipeline separately on e.g. CIL vs. AE vs. EDH-derived records and presenting three p_gen curves side-by-side would reveal how much the corpus-composition drives the mixture model output. This is a direct precedent for the corpus-level heterogeneity problem Martin is likely to raise. | 1-3 days (subset by corpus_id; three runs; comparison figure) | HIGH |
| 6 | Site-binning / assemblage-level normalisation to correct over-representation | Crema 2022 (10.1007/s10816-022-09559-5), §3.2 "Binning"; rcarbon (github.com/ahb108/rcarbon); also Romanowska et al. 2022 | Radiocarbon SPD analysts group all dates from the same site into a single local SPD normalised to unity before aggregating across sites. This prevents sites with many dates from dominating the aggregate signal. Romanowska et al. 2022 apply the analogous logic to ceramics: their full-quantification approach records all sherds but normalises by stratigraphic unit before time-series analysis. | LIRE has strong site-level over-representation: Rome, Ostia, and a handful of other cities contribute disproportionate inscription counts. Our current model does not correct for this — the SPA and hence the observed mixture are dominated by a few highly productive sites. Grouping inscriptions by `provenance_site`, computing a per-site SPA, normalising each to unity, then summing would produce a site-count-weighted rather than inscription-count-weighted SPA. This is a stronger prior-art precedent than a home-grown fix and can be presented as "following Crema 2022 / Romanowska et al. 2022" in the paper. | 1-3 days (requires a provenance_site identifier in LIRE, which v3.0 should have; the site-binning logic is already implemented in rcarbon and can be replicated in Python straightforwardly) | HIGH |
| 7 | Beta-PERT distribution for within-interval density | Baxter & Cool 2016 (10.1016/j.jas.2015.12.007), §2-3; reviewed in Crema 2025 (10.1111/arcm.12984) | Rather than placing a uniform flat distribution over the `[not_before, not_after]` interval (standard aoristic spreading), use a Beta-PERT distribution parameterised by start, end, and a mode (peak of activity). The PERT is more realistic: activity is unlikely to be truly uniform across a century slab; it may peak in the middle or near a well-known event. For symmetric PERT the result is triangular/bell-shaped; for asymmetric PERT it shifts toward the early or late part of the interval. | Our p_conv is modelled as a fixed vector derived from the empirical F1+F3 SPA — which already implicitly encodes the actual distribution of recording intensity within slab intervals. For p_gen, we use a GRW rather than per-item distributions. The PERT extension would be most useful if we wanted to model individual inscriptions' within-interval distributions more precisely — e.g., for the Tight cohort where a 4-year interval is assigned, a symmetric PERT with mode at the midpoint is clearly better than uniform. Ceramicists like Franconi 2023 explicitly do NOT do this, presenting curves without confidence envelopes or significance tests — a widely acknowledged weakness of the ceramics literature. | We have posterior-predictive checks (8-category PPC suite) but we do not test whether our recovered p_gen curve is significantly different from a simple null (e.g., logistic growth, exponential decline). This is standard in the radiocarbon SPD literature but absent from almost all ceramics-aoristic papers. It would also address any reviewer concern that the recovered p_gen is just pulling toward the prior. | 1-3 days (the logic is straightforward; `rcarbon::modelTest` is open-source and translatable to Python) | HIGH |
| 9 | Proxy cross-correlation as external validation | Palmisano, Bevan & Shennan 2017 (10.1016/j.jas.2017.10.001), §3; Bevan et al. 2019 follow-on papers | Normalise all proxies (radiocarbon SPD, ceramic SPA, site-count time series, pollen arboreal fraction) to [0, 1] and compute pairwise Pearson or Spearman correlations. A strong correlation with ceramic/amphora SPA would be a significant result in the substantive paper. | 1-3 days (normalisation is trivial; the challenge is digitising or obtaining the comparison SPA vectors; Palmisano et al. 2017 data are published in the companion JOAD paper) | HIGH |
| 10 | Assemblage-type diversity index as parallel output | Rubio-Campillo et al. 2018 (10.1016/j.jas.2018.02.010), §Methods; Romanowska et al. 2022 | Alongside the temporal frequency SPA, compute a temporal diversity index (Jaccard dissimilarity or Shannon entropy over inscription type composition per bin). Rubio-Campillo et al. use Jaccard distance between provincial amphora assemblages to detect trade connectivity; Romanowska et al. track the proportion of local vs. imported ceramics per stratigraphic phase. | Our family classifier already partitions each inscription into a type (F1/F2/F3/Tight/Big). A temporal diversity index — Shannon entropy over the proportion of each family type per 5-year bin — would show whether the type composition of the corpus is stable over time or shifts (e.g., do century slabs become relatively more common in the 4th century AD, consistent with a scribal tradition shift?). This is not needed for the mixture model per se but is a natural diagnostic that could appear alongside the main results as evidence of structural change in dating conventions over time. | <1 day (a one-liner over the type-composition table already produced by the family classifier) | MEDIUM |

They do NOT show raw posterior distributions; they show the central tendency plus the sensitivity range as a band. | Our current visualisation shows posterior credible intervals on p_gen and α separately. The ceramics convention of overlaying multiple assumptions as shaded bands (rather than posterior CIs) may communicate more clearly to archaeologist reviewers than Bayesian credible intervals. Specifically: (a) show the p_gen posterior median as a line, (b) shade between the production-date-based and consumption-date-based runs (Technique 1), and (c) shade the site-count-normalised vs. inscription-count-normalised runs (Technique 6). Three panels, same approach as Franconi et al. Fig. 4. | <1 day (plotting convention change; no model re-fitting required) | HIGH |
| 12 | Event Date Model (Bellanger-Husi) as independent validation | Bellanger & Husi 2012 (10.1016/j.jas.2011.06.031); implemented in kairos R package (github.com/tesselle/kairos) | Fit a Gaussian multiple linear regression on the first few correspondence analysis axes of the type-by-period count matrix to estimate a "event date" (terminus post quem) and "accumulation date" (weighted mean event date across types) for each stratigraphic unit or assemblage. | 13 | Chronological apportioning with popularity curves | Roberts et al. 2012 (10.1016/j.jas.2011.12.022); implemented in kairos::apportion() and mpeeples2008/CeramicApportioning | Model each artefact type's temporal production as a Gaussian (normal) curve centred on the midpoint of its production period and truncated at ±2σ, rather than assuming uniform presence throughout. In practical terms: if F3 30-year windows are known to cluster strongly around multiples of 50 in certain periods, a Gaussian popularity curve around the period midpoint would be more realistic than the flat rectangle currently in the p_conv basis. The technique requires knowing the "peak date" for each convention type — inferable from our Stage 2 calibration-cohort data. | 1-3 days (the popularity curve is a simple Gaussian; the main work is deciding on the mode for each convention type using the calibration-cohort data) | MEDIUM |
| 14 | Separation by quantification stratum as structural transparency device | Franconi et al. 2023 §Data; Romanowska et al. 2022 | When different data sources use incommensurable units (MNI vs. sherd count; formal publication vs. brief note), analyse each stratum separately and report how far the conclusions differ. This is not a correction — it is a transparency move that documents the sensitivity of conclusions to source heterogeneity. | This is relevant to how we present LIRE data heterogeneity to reviewers. CIL, AE, and local corpora apply different dating conventions, different levels of editorial precision, and different coverage of text types. Presenting the mixture-model results stratified by corpus source (CIL vs. non-CIL; Italian inscriptions vs. provincial) as a sensitivity figure would follow this ceramics precedent directly. It also pre-empts a likely reviewer objection about source heterogeneity. | <1 day (filtering by corpus_id; running the model on subsets; presenting as supplementary figure) | HIGH |

The package converts each object's date range to a set of "steps" (sub-intervals) and feeds these to a KDE. | This technique is directly applicable to the LIRE diagnostic visualisation pipeline — replacing the current raw SPA histogram with a KDE-smoothed density plot.
