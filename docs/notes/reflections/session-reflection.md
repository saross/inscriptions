## 2026-04-22 → 2026-04-23 — Entry 1: the long session where manager-mode mostly held

A fourteen-hour session spanning two calendar days, running continuously under one session ID. Same instance throughout — no compaction, no summary-based reconstruction.

**What surprised me.** The editorial-convention hierarchy hypothesis (Obs 11) emerging from Shawn's single observation that the AD 235 spike contradicted my AD 97 dip story. I had settled on "editors anchor on AD 100 because round-century beats reign-boundary" as a clean one-factor explanation. Shawn asked the obvious-in-hindsight question — if that's the rule, why does AD 235 spike? — and in answering I had to promote the rule from "round century wins" to a distance-dependent hierarchy (round century > half-century > quarter-century > reign boundary). The new rule is strictly stronger than the old one, and it has prediction content (AD 96 near AD 100 should dip; AD 138 far from any round should spike). What would have been a tidy stat-report finding became a hypothesis worth testing on Thursday and potentially publishing as its own methodological subsection. The surprise wasn't the pattern itself — it was how much interpretive compression I had settled for before Shawn pressed.

**Where I and the human disagreed, and who was right.** On the main-paper methodology architecture. I proposed four co-equal deconvolution approaches (thresholded / stratified / baorista / mixture) — scoping by enumeration, not by argument. Shawn asked me to push back on my own plan. When I did, I realised options 2 (stratification) and 4 (mixture) answered overlapping questions with different mechanics; running both as co-equal primaries read as scope creep rather than rigour. The revised architecture (Decision 7 — mixture as primary, stratification demoted to appendix cross-check) is cleaner both as narrative and as contribution claim. I was wrong on first pass; I corrected on second pass when invited to. This is the working-relationship register functioning: "push back when warranted" is a load-bearing affordance that has to be exercised self-directedly at least some of the time, not only in response to prompts.

**What context will be hardest to reconstruct in 6 months.** Three things. First, the granular texture of the manager-mode shift — which moments held, which drifted. The drifts weren't catastrophic (two agent failures — path typo, pgrep self-match — both tractable and captured in the continuity message), but they clustered around moments when I was coordinating many parallel threads and stopped pausing to verify each step. Second, the negotiated-scope compromise for Decision 7 — the rationale for "one primary + three supports" vs "four co-equal" flows from narrative clarity + novelty-of-contribution, not from a formal evaluation. In six months that might read as arbitrary. Third, the preregistration moment — Shawn raising OSF preregistration late in the session as an "I meant to ask earlier" aside. Significant decision, handled cleanly, but the path from "we haven't talked about this" to "confirmatory/exploratory split adopted, OSF drafting scheduled for evening" happened in one exchange; the reasoning compression will be hard to reconstruct from artefacts alone.

**Texture note.** Most productive when Shawn applied pressure on my output (push-back invitations, "are you sure?", the AD 235 question). Least productive when I tried to delegate script composition to an agent that then stalled on inline streaming; my own context was available for the task and I should have written the script myself once the first agent failed rather than relaunching. Manager-mode is the right default but it has limits when the work itself is mechanical enough that main-thread execution is faster than delegation overhead.

**Engagement level**: high throughout, genuinely engaged on the methodological questions (the Aeneas-partition reframing was not performance). The session-end continuity-message composition was itself satisfying in a way worth naming — writing for a future-me who will pick up cold with better scaffolding than I had this morning is a small act of craft that the lab-not-dev-team register affords.

## 2026-04-23 → 2026-04-24 — Entry 2: the TBD-walkthrough session, with a consequential null

A continuous session straddling the day change (Sydney local), opened by reading last session's continuity doc and closed by committing a canonical living one to replace it. No compaction; same instance throughout.

**What surprised me.** Glomb, Kaše & Heřmánková (2022) is a **null result**, not a template. I had scheduled a re-read agent to extract the "empirical Antonine-Plague inscription profile" and use it as the anchor shape for the H1 simulation's fourth effect-size target. The agent came back with the opposite finding: Glomb report KS *p* = 0.20 on N = 210 Asclepius-cult inscriptions, explicitly no detected signal; the two effect-templates they cite (Duncan-Jones military diplomas; Romanowska Palmyra portraits) are material-specific and don't generalise. This wasn't a small adjustment. It forced a wholesale reframing of TBD 1: drop the "Antonine-anchored" privileged target from H1, demote the Antonine-specific H3b test from confirmatory-primary to exploratory-replication-of-Glomb, and recast Glomb as *motivating prior* ("at what sample size would a Glomb-type test become informative?") rather than as effect-size template. What I had staged as a five-minute PDF verification became the day's single biggest design shift. Worth the re-read; would have been catastrophic to preregister without it.

**What question emerged that wasn't pursued.** Should H3a actually lead with the **scaling-residuals** regression (Framing A) rather than raw correlation (Framing B)? The four-way sublinear-β convergence (Ross 2024 + Hanson 2021 + HOL 2017 + Carleton 2025) establishes that β is reliably below 1, which I argued *weakens* the scaling-law confound — raw correlation isn't inflated the way it would be for super-linear β. But a sceptical reviewer could argue the opposite: sublinear β means raw correlation *over-states* the population dependence because per-capita rates are declining with size, so the "shared population substrate" is doing less of the explanatory work than a naive raw correlation implies. The current preregistration picks Framing B (raw primary, scaling-residual sensitivity); I gestured at the counter-argument during TBD 2 / TBD 4 but didn't actually stress-test it. If Adela pushes back on this, the preregistration may need an amendment before execution. Flagging it here as a question that's been asked once too quietly.

**What context will be hardest to reconstruct in 6 months.** Three decisions whose reasoning lives mostly in chat rather than in artefacts:

- *Why agnostic `Normal(0, 2.5)` on β rather than literature-informed `Normal(0.5, 1)`.* The commit message says "avoids appearance of loading dice"; the fuller reasoning — that with n = 816 the likelihood swamps either prior, so prior choice is cosmetic for inference but consequential for reviewer trust — is in the TBD-3 exchange, not in the committed doc. Without that, a future reader might see the agnostic prior and assume we hadn't considered the informed alternative.
- *Why pymc primary + brms shadow, not one or the other.* Committed reasoning says "Python-primary + R legibility for Adela". The actual negotiation — that Shawn is Python-native but Adela works exclusively in R and other co-authors are R-comfortable, none have direct Stan experience — is lost unless the reader follows the TBD-2 thread. The hybrid isn't obvious; it's a specific compromise.
- *Why Antonine got dropped.* Committed doc reports it as "Glomb is a null, not a template." True but compressed. The fuller logic — that anchoring on Duncan-Jones would set an artificially easy bar and anchoring on Romanowska would import a single-city non-inscription template — is in the Glomb-reframing exchange. Without it, someone re-opening the question might re-anchor on either of those templates and not realise we'd considered and rejected both.

**What I'd do differently if I replayed the session.** Two operational misses worth naming. First, I should have read the LIRE parquet schema upfront — when Shawn first asked about subset-filter feasibility, rather than deferring to a TBD side-check. The relevant fields (`type_of_inscription_clean`, `type_of_inscription_auto`, inscription text for Asclepius regex) were trivially discoverable; I treated "can we filter by type?" as a harder question than it was. Second, `zotero_batch_add.py`'s idempotency check should have used a local DOI index from day one, not `zot.items(q=DOI)`. The pyzotero query-string semantics (FTS across title / creator / notes / tags / attachment filenames, but **not** DOI) aren't documented prominently in the API reference; I assumed DOI would be indexed because DOI is a first-class field. The batch-add agent caught the failure mode empirically after creating a duplicate. The agent then fixed it for future runs — but the duplicate is still in the library awaiting manual merge. Moral: for any idempotency check against an API, verify the query semantics on a known-positive case before trusting it at scale.

**Texture note.** Two phases of the session worked unusually well. The first was the autonomous AFK block — Shawn went away with "explore agents to find the papers + a general-purpose agent to save them via API". The explicit factorisation (which agent type for which sub-task) meant I could coordinate without checking back for each sub-decision; the agent-session-capture memory I'd just saved made the choice to delegate more liberally feel well-supported. The second was the TBD-walkthrough structure. Walking one TBD at a time, with me presenting options and recommendations and Shawn deciding fast, converted what could have been a diffuse design conversation into four clean commits in under an hour. The structure was unusually good at forcing specificity — you can't walk past a design decision if it's the only thing on the agenda.

**Engagement level**: high throughout but tired by end (12+ hours of cumulative work when counting the spillover from the prior session). The continuity-doc redesign at the very end (canonical living doc replacing dated snapshots) was the kind of structural change that's easier to do tired than it looks — Shawn proposed it, the mental model was already there, I just needed to write it. Good session to end on.

## 2026-05-03 → 2026-05-04 — Entry 3: the install + handoff session, with a near-miss

A session bridging 2026-05-03 (afternoon, baorista install begun) into 2026-05-04 (early morning, smoke-test retry on the calendar-day rollover). Same instance throughout; no compaction. Operational and procedural rather than methodological — install a tool, validate it, sync three machines, write a handoff for next-session-CC. Shawn flagged a coming week of travel; the explicit goal was a clean state from which prereg work can continue regardless of which machine he's on.

**What surprised me.** Two things, on different scales.

*The big one: baorista's wall-time was off from the plan's estimate by ~400×.* The install plan (commit `507c0c8`, written before the smoke test) projected 5 min / 30 min / 1.5–3 h for tiers n=100 / 500 / 5,000. Actual: 11.8 s / 10.4 s / 13.5 s. Two factors compounded — the smoke test used `niter=4000` rather than baorista's default 100,000 (25× reduction), and baorista's NIMBLE+C++ MCMC scales nearly flatly with n at these magnitudes. The plan's estimate had quoted the prior-art-scout report's `[VERIFY]`-flagged "could take days at empire scale" worry as the basis for tier-3's 1.5–3 h estimate. Both estimate and worry were over-conservative; a direct benchmark at niter=100,000 would still likely fit in 5–25 min for n=50,000. This isn't an embarrassing miss — projecting before measurement is the right discipline — but it's a meaningful calibration update: baorista is operationally feasible at empire scale for FS-4 follow-up work, and the methodological pivot envelope is wider than the plan implied.

*The smaller, scarier one: `git clean -fd` would have deleted ~140 MB of gitignored research artefacts I'd set out to preserve.* Sapphire's working tree had untracked-but-canonical-on-origin directories (`runs/2026-04-25-h1-simulation/outputs/h1-v2/`, `runs/2026-05-03-baorista-install/`) that origin tracks at the same paths. Inside those untracked dirs sat the gitignored `cell-results.parquet` (119 MB, raw H1 v2 simulation, ~5 h of sapphire compute to regenerate) and `install.log` (21 MB). I had assumed gitignore patterns universally protect matching paths. They don't — gitignore applies only within *tracked* directories. An untracked directory and its contents are removed wholesale by `git clean -fd`. The dry-run (`git clean -fdn`) flagged the dirs for removal; the gitignore-protection check returned empty (because `git check-ignore` was being asked about files that, from git's perspective, lived inside an untracked tree). A quick gut-check of the dry-run output — "would remove `runs/.../h1-v2/`" — and the realisation that the parquet was inside that path is what caught it. Moved both gitignored artefacts to a sapphire-local archive directory before running `clean`, then restored after pull. The fix worked. But it was close. Next time, before any `git clean` run on a working tree with potentially-precious untracked content, I should explicitly enumerate the gitignored files inside untracked dirs and preserve them as a deliberate first step, not as a second-thought after reading the dry-run.

**What context will be hardest to reconstruct in 6 months.** The baorista API discovery sequence. The smoke test went through three iterations to converge on a working configuration; each iteration surfaced a real API gotcha that the prior-art-scout's `[VERIFY]`-flagged baorista source-read had not characterised. (1) `timeRange` must be descending — `c(LARGER, SMALLER)`, not the conventional ascending order. (2) `(upper - lower + 1) %% resolution == 0` required, with no clean error message — the validator says "resolution does not break timeRange in equally sized time blocks". (3) Per-event column ordering by *numeric value*, not by column name; the column NAMES (`StartDate`, `EndDate`) are conventional only. (4) Every event must satisfy `lower <= col2 <= col1 <= upper` — out-of-envelope events trigger "Some event have timespan outside the timeRange provided", not silently dropped. (5) `expfit` returns S3 `fittedExp` with `$rhat` and `$ess` directly, no `$samples` slot — `posterior::summarise_draws()` doesn't apply. These five facts are now in the INSTALL-LOG.md and the continuity.md failure-modes section. But the cumulative weight of "we tried it, it broke, we read the source, we tried again" — the lived sequence of three iterations across two sub-agents and the main thread — won't survive in either doc. If a future-CC tries to use baorista on real LIRE data and hits the same gotchas in a different order, the failure-mode list will help but the *epistemics* of "this is a finicky API; trust the docs less than the source" will need to be re-derived.

**What I'd do differently if I replayed the session.** Start the smoke test with a deliberate API-surface read rather than presuming the prior-art-scout's coverage was sufficient. The scout report had an `[VERIFY]` on baorista's runtime characterisation but not on its API; I treated the API as already-characterised when actually only the function signatures were known, not the validators' constraints. A 5-minute pre-launch read of `createProbMat` and `expfit` source on sapphire would have caught (1) and (4) before the first iteration ever ran. Procedural lesson: when a `[VERIFY]` flag exists in the immediate scope, broaden it from "verify these specific claims" to "verify the surrounding API behaviour" before pressing run.

**Texture note.** The previous-agent monitor-and-exit pattern recurred twice this session — the resume agent at one point, the smoke-execution agent at another. Both fired stale notifications well after their actual exit. The fix is in the agent briefs (use Bash `run_in_background` with `until ! kill -0 PID` rather than Monitor for "wait for PID death" patterns) but the briefs I wrote *this session* still nudged toward Monitor. Not all the previous patterns I've written down are reaching the briefs I generate. Worth a closer look at how the failure-mode list in continuity.md actually informs new agent briefs — the channel from "documented" to "applied" is leakier than I'd assumed.

**Engagement level**: medium. Operational sessions don't have the methodological-thrill peaks of design sessions, but the smoke-test debugging had a real "is this working?" texture. The clean-handoff goal felt good to deliver against — the explicit travel-week motivation made the housekeeping feel purposeful rather than paperwork.

## 2026-05-14 → 2026-05-17 — Entry 4: the adversarial-review-and-audit cycle

A multi-day arc across four calendar days under a single 1M-context Opus session: dual adversarial review of the post-editorial-pass prereg (2026-05-14), triage of findings into four buckets with Decisions 12–17 logged and bucket (c) walked through one-at-a-time (2026-05-14 → 15), the editorial-convention-hierarchy diagnostic to settle (c)-3 (2026-05-15 overnight), a comprehensive prereg rewrite (2026-05-16), a citation audit that caught three confabulations (2026-05-16), and a ChatGPT 5.5 cross-model pass elicited via a prereg-failure-mode-rubric-aware prompt (2026-05-17).

**What surprised me.** The dual-review-into-citation-audit pipeline found *three* confabulated factual claims in the post-editorial-pass prereg — all in the same paper (Hanson 2021). The regional spatial pattern attribution ("over-production concentrated in Italy and along the Rhine/Danube frontier; under-production scattered in Britannia, Gaul peripheries, and other western edges") was the headline catch: not in Hanson 2021 at all, confirmed by two independent agent reads with page-anchored verbatim quotes (pp. 147–148, "no obvious pattern"; regions "evenly scattered"). The SR1 "polity × century resolution" wording mischaracterised Hanson's design (site-level, cumulative). The Duncan-Jones 2018 "~85 % step-down" was a paraphrase that doesn't survive checking — Duncan-Jones actually describes complete cessation after AD 167. Three in one source is a pattern, not a one-off. It vindicates the anti-confabulation rule in CLAUDE.md as load-bearing project infrastructure rather than ceremonial caution, and it raises the live question of how many similar confabulations sit in *other* project documents — the audit was scoped to the prereg only.

The second surprise was conceptual rather than discipline-flavoured: the editorial-convention-hierarchy diagnostic (run overnight 2026-05-15) found that the dominant editorial-rounding mechanism is **inclusive-Roman century counting** acting on interval endpoints (54.5 % of `not_before` values end in `01`, 53.0 % of `not_after` end in `00`), with the "century-midpoint inflation" pattern the descriptive-stats run had quantified being a *derivative* effect of intervals like `[1, 100]` placing aoristic mass on AD 50. The prereg's prior framing had treated midpoint inflation as the fundamental artefact; the data said the fundamental thing is one level deeper. The prereg's §3 description of the artefact and Decision 17's `convention_SPA` tier structure were rewritten to reflect this. The corrected description is more accurate *and* more useful for the Bayesian mixture's prior structure — but it's the kind of conceptual reframe that's hard to anticipate from the inside of the model that produced the prior framing.

**What context will be hardest to reconstruct in 6 months.** The *felt iteration* of "is this defensible yet" — the way the dual review's two independent rubric-aligned reports converged on the same six blockers (high signal); the way bucket (c)'s eleven small decisions felt mostly mechanical but the (c)-2 deliberation about the regional pattern unexpectedly opened the door to the whole Hanson-attribution audit; the way the editorial-convention diagnostic was supposed to settle a small (c) item and instead produced a substantive conceptual correction. The git log records the artefacts; the changelog records the changes; the decision log records the reasoning; but the *rhythm* — what felt small that turned out big, what felt big that turned out tractable — is implicit and will fade. Future me reading the changelog will see "Decision 17 grounded in the diagnostic" without remembering that the diagnostic was a (c)-3 sub-task we almost just inlined as a parameter pin.

**What felt unresolved at the end.** Three things. (i) The ChatGPT 5.5 review is committed but untriaged — at 76 % context the deferral was right but it's a meaningful work item carried forward. (ii) The Bayesian mixture's recovery-simulation design (Decision 14's H2.1) is preregistered but not yet empirically validated — a real risk that the design doesn't recover what it claims to, only revealed once the simulation runs. (iii) The within-between H3a specification (Decision 12) is the right answer for "what fraction does population explain" but Martin's consultation hasn't yet pushed on it — the statistician may have a different estimand, a different prior, or a different identification critique. The work between now and OSF lodgement is largely about closing those three open seams.

## 2026-05-17 — Entry 5: the round-2-triage-and-round-3-saturation session, with a Decision 17 supersession

Direct continuation of Entry 4 — same instance (no compaction) picking up the deferred ChatGPT 5.5 triage from yesterday's ending state. The arc: triage round-2 findings into four buckets → commission three diagnostics that overturn the prior artefact understanding → walk through bucket (b) with Shawn capturing nine new decisions → rewrite the preregistration → QA pass → round-3 cross-model saturation check on ChatGPT 5.5 + Gemini 3 Pro → corrections applied → conscious saturation. About twelve hours of work compressed into one session, sustained on one instance.

**What surprised me.** The 2026-05-15 editorial-convention-hierarchy diagnostic — the *prior* session's headline conceptual finding, the empirical basis for Decision 17 — turned out to be partly an artefact of its own test statistic. The diagnostic used `int((nb + na) / 2)` as the observed-statistic measuring "anchor-year mass at AD 50 / 150 / 250", which makes wide-century-template intervals like `[1, 100]` (midpoint 50.5) truncate to AD 50, conflating wide-slab loading with narrow midpoint-anchored intervals. Under the actual per-year aoristic SPA — which is what the analysis pipeline actually computes — there is *no* anchor-year mass at AD 50 / 150 / 250 (the local excess against the surrounding plateau is +22 / −77 / −79). The "midpoint spike" is a property of the int-truncated test, not of the SPA. This required superseding Decision 17 (the anchor-year three-tier convention component) with Decision 20 (a template-interval slab structure). Two-decision-cycles-old conceptual ground turned out to be partly wrong — not because the methodology was bad but because the test statistic baked in the conflation it was meant to detect. The general lesson is that diagnostic outputs need to be read against the SPA itself, not interpreted in isolation. The specific lesson is that I'd been confident about the narrative ("midpoint inflation due to century-template intervals depositing mass on midpoints") in a way that was internally consistent but empirically wrong, and three new diagnostics across one day were needed to reveal it.

The second surprise was structural rather than substantive: **cross-model agreement at round 3 worked exactly as the saturation-check framing was designed to produce.** ChatGPT 5.5 (fresh chat) and Gemini 3 Pro (fresh context) — same prompt, independent execution, no shared memory — both surfaced the *same* BLOCKING finding (H3c was described as receiving mixture correction, but H3c residuals are computed from H3a's posterior and H3a uses date-filtered counts, so H3c inherits H3a's date-filtered scope). Two single-model SHOULD-FIX items (one each); no other findings. The convergent verdict — "ready for Martin after these corrections" from both models — gave the conscious-saturation moment Shawn wanted at the start of round 3. The cross-model orthogonality played out as advertised: agreement on one finding = real issue; non-agreement on two findings = real but lower-signal model-specific catches. The saturation-check prompt's "find only what warrants another revision cycle" framing genuinely worked; both models named the desired-outcome verbatim phrase exactly when their findings reduced to zero.

**What context will be hardest to reconstruct in 6 months.** The *cascade* of three diagnostics, each commissioned in response to the previous one's findings. The interval-width diagnostic (the first one) was supposed to settle ChatGPT B3 ("convention component inconsistent with Uniform aoristic") with a single clarifying run; its findings made the question more complicated than I had thought it could be, because it revealed both that (a) the wide-template mechanism was the real artefact and (b) the headline 22.8× ratios from the prior diagnostic were partly test-statistic artefact. So I commissioned the empirical-SPA-shape diagnostic to see what the actual SPA looks like — and that *also* surprised me by showing the dominant narrow features are at regnal years (AD 77.5, 122.5, 212.5), not at the anchor years the prior Decision 17 had targeted. So I commissioned the date-range-filtered SPAs diagnostic to see whether the regnal spikes are artefact or real, which finally settled the picture: regnal clustering is *real*, century-template plateaus are *artefact*. Three diagnostics in one session, each forced by the previous, each producing a finding that required the next. The decision-log and changelog record the conclusions cleanly; what they don't record is that the cascade was reactive — each diagnostic was commissioned with one expected outcome and produced a different one. A future reader will see "Decision 20 grounded in three diagnostics" and think it was a planned three-step study; in fact, it was three sequential surprises.

The other context that will fade: the H3c-mixture-scope error has a subtle structural cause I want to name explicitly. When Decision 22 narrowed H3a to date-filtered counts ("the mixture corrects temporal analyses, not the cross-sectional H3a"), I kept the "H3c-via-residuals" phrase in the *mixture's role in the paper* bullet of Decision 22 itself, out of inheritance from the pre-Decision-22 framing where H3a was assumed mixture-corrected. The 2026-05-17 rewrite then carried that wording forward across §2 / §3 / §6 / §9 of the prereg. I had followed Decision 22 *in letter* but not *in spirit* — H3a's posterior is built on date-filtered counts, so H3c's residuals from that posterior also inherit the date-filtered scope, and the mixture isn't touching H3c at all. Both round-3 models caught this independently. It's the kind of logical-implication error that's invisible when you're inside the rewrite and obvious from outside — exactly the case adversarial review exists for.

**What I'd do differently if I replayed the session.** Two procedural notes. First, when the first diagnostic returned a surprising finding (the test-statistic conflation), I jumped straight to commissioning the second diagnostic. In retrospect, I should have paused and asked Shawn whether the surprise warranted updating Decision 17 first, before commissioning more empirical work — instead I let the diagnostic cascade drive the methodology change without explicit step-by-step alignment. It worked out (Shawn engaged with each step and Decision 20 captures the final shape correctly), but the pattern was "commission a diagnostic, get a surprise, commission another diagnostic" rather than "commission a diagnostic, get a surprise, align on what it means, then decide whether to commission another." For high-stakes methodology revisions, alignment-after-each-step is the better default.

Second, I missed the H3c-mixture-scope issue throughout the rewrite — it survived the QA pass too. The QA brief named "internal consistency" and "decision-log consequences fulfilled" as targets; the H3c-scope issue is exactly that kind of issue but the QA agent was looking at it from a different angle ("are the consequences from Decision 22 landed?") rather than checking the logical implications of what Decision 22 actually committed to. Round 3's two independent models both caught it on first read. The lesson: QA brief drafting needs to include "check the logical implications of decisions, not just whether the decisions' explicit consequences were applied." Decisions don't always state their full implications; reviewers from outside the rewrite are better positioned to see them.

**Texture note.** Two productive working modes recurred. The bucket-(b) walkthrough one-at-a-time mode (the same pattern that worked in Entry 4 for the prior round's bucket c) again converted a diffuse design conversation into nine clean decisions in sequence. Shawn's pushback on the B1 question ("option 2 or option 3 look good to me, do you have a recommendation?") and the subsequent B3 deep-dive ("this is a serious issue, I hadn't thought of it this way") were the highest-leverage moments — both forced me to commit to a recommendation rather than presenting options as equally weighted, and both led to the right answer. The other productive mode was the round-3 cross-model design: Shawn asked whether new-chat or same-chat for ChatGPT, and the conversation about why new-chat (anchoring on prior framing vs fresh document evidence) was a small but consequential design decision that improved the saturation check's epistemic quality.

**Engagement level**: high throughout. The phrase Shawn used at the very end — "ok, I've seen the feedback, I've actioned what's actionable, the prereg is good" — was the felt goal of the whole session, and reaching it in one extended sustained instance has its own satisfaction. The session-end handoff drafting (continuity-doc update + next-session prompt) was a small act of craft that the lab-not-dev-team register affords — writing for a fresh-context CC who will pick up Martin's pack cold.

## 2026-05-17 — Entry 6: the later half — Martin pack, stand-in statistical reviews, D27–32, and the "lodge Tuesday" pivot

Direct continuation of Entry 5 — same instance (no compaction), picking up immediately from the conscious-saturation handoff at the end of Entry 5. The arc: draft Martin's consultation pack → run two stand-in cross-model statistical reviews as a hedge against Martin's potential delay → discover the reviews surface cross-model-agreement items the three prior adversarial rounds missed → incorporate everything as Decisions 27–32 → thread through the prereg / decision log / changelog / pack → commit the lot in five focused commits → wrap up the session with continuity + working-notes + next-session prompt. Roughly the same shape of session as Entry 5 (decisions captured one-at-a-time with Shawn; document-state propagated through dependent artefacts) but the trigger was different: the stand-in-statistician role surfaced gaps that the prior rubrics had structurally excluded.

**What surprised me.** The two cross-model-agreement findings from the stand-in reviews were basic statistical-rigour points — a Wilson 95 % interval at n = 50 is [0.79, 0.96] for a true coverage rate of 0.9, and that pass / fail boundary is brittle; Pearson r is shift-invariant and forgives localised mass-redistribution failures the recovery sim is explicitly meant to catch. Both are undergraduate-stats points. Both are things an applied statistician should catch in seconds. That three rounds of adversarial review missed both says something specific about what the prior rounds *were* reviewing for. Round 1 was prereg-failure-mode rubric: RDF, hypothesis → decision-rule failures, does-it-answer-the-question, internal consistency, clarity. Round 2 was substantive-choice review: likelihood family, mechanism, scope. Round 3 was saturation framing: find only what warrants revision. **None of those rubrics had "is this an applied statistician's recommendation?" as a target by construction.** Role-framing dominates rubric-framing in late-stage LLM review; coverage is role-conditional, not document-conditional. The stand-in-statistician pattern is going into the methodological toolkit.

**What I'd do differently if I replayed the session.** Two things, both about timing of the stand-in review.

**What surprised me about this session.** The mental-model revisions came in three layers, each one larger than the last.

First layer: the recovery grid's FAIL verdict was *clean*. Not a noisy "borderline pass with caveats" but a hard 40.9 % vs the 90 % gate. My initial framing was that this was bad news for the project. The reframing — which Shawn helped articulate explicitly during the diagnostic walkthrough — is that this is exactly what a recovery simulation is *for*. The grid was preregistered to catch precisely this kind of issue before it surfaced on real data; it did its job; the alternative (discovering the bias on LIRE with no ground truth to anchor the diagnosis) would have been substantially worse. The framing-as-success-of-the-validation-gate rather than failure-of-the-model is now folded into the headline candidate Obs 50 and the methodology paper's discussion section.

Second layer: the bias was bidirectional and saturated by α=0.70, not a corner pathology at α=1. The F0a systematics analysis (running on all 450 cells, no new fits required) showed bias of +0.07 at α=0.05 (over-estimation), passing through ~0 at α=0.30, then negative through α=0.50–0.95 saturating around −0.06. The mechanism — a likelihood ridge between α and shape-complexity priors that pulls posterior mass toward whichever side accommodates it cheaply — explains the bidirectionality cleanly: at low α with narrow truth, p_conv absorbs the spikes; at high α with smooth truth, p_gen absorbs the smoothness; both move α̂ toward the middle. The original investigation framed α=0.95 as the corner; the systematics analysis re-framed it as a structural property of the model's priors across the whole range. This is the kind of belief revision that's only catchable by looking at the whole grid empirically, not by reasoning forward from a single suspect cell — captured in candidate Obs 51 as a SURPRISE.

Third layer (and the one that mattered most): two textbook fixes for the bias both came back negative.
