# Discard vs recover — should we throw away the editorial 2/3 of LIRE, or recover signal from it?

A devil's-advocate analysis of whether the mixture-model effort is
worth it. Written after the family-classifier results (2026-05-24)
made the editorial-vs-signal split quantitatively visible:
60.7 % editorial round-number slabs (F1), 4.5 % editorial periodic
windows (F3), 17.4 % wide non-round intervals (Big — likely-editorial),
9.6 % reign-window content (F2_Other — signal-but-aoristic), 7.8 %
tight datings (Tight — hard signal). Strict count: ~17 % signal,
~83 % editorial-or-wide.

## The question

LIRE is dominated by editorial dating, not intrinsic signal. The
mixture-model approach we've been developing tries to **extract
partial information from every inscription**, treating the editorial
convention as a learnable component to be subtracted from the
observed counts. The discard approach instead **throws away the
editorial-templated inscriptions** and analyses only the signal subset
(7.8 % tight + 9.6 % reign-window = 17.4 % at most, ~ 32,000 records).

Two real options. Trade-offs in both directions. Which is the right
call for the Hanson-scaling paper and the methodological contribution?

## The case FOR discarding the editorial majority

1. **The recovery grid told us the mixture model has a structural
   α-bias.** Until we have a validated structural fix (the empirical-
   Bayes calibration cohort being the leading candidate), the
   mixture-model output for α is biased downward by ~ 0.06 at any
   α ≥ 0.30. The discard approach gives noisier-but-unbiased
   estimates.
2. **Simplicity is a scientific virtue.** Any future researcher with
   basic Python can replicate "filter to narrow-dated subset, run
   SPA". Defending the mixture-model results requires defending the
   prior, the convention basis, the smoothness assumptions, the
   reweighting, the σ priors, the convergence diagnostics. A hostile
   reviewer has many surfaces to attack.
3. **17 % of 180,609 records is still 30,000+ inscriptions** —
   large by Mediterranean epigraphy standards. Many published studies
   in the field run on 1,000-10,000 records.
4. **For some specific historical questions, discard is just *better*.**
   Hanson scaling on cities with enough urban-area data: those cities
   also have enough tight-dated inscriptions. Specific event tests
   (Antonine plague, Crisis of Third Century, dated imperial campaigns):
   the tight-dated subset is sufficient.
5. **It generalises cleanly to other corpora.** EDR, EDH, EDCS, EAGLE
   all have similar editorial conventions but in different proportions.
   Discard-and-analyse is portable. The mixture-model approach with
   empirical-Bayes calibration would need re-validation for each
   corpus.

## The case AGAINST discarding (i.e., for the mixture-model effort)

1. **Editorial convention is not zero information.** When an editor
   writes "AD 101-200", they are not saying "I have no idea when this
   is from" — they are saying "this is from the second century, almost
   certainly not the first or third". That rules out 75 % of the LIRE
   timeline. The mixture model is the formalism for extracting that
   partial information; throwing it away is statistically wasteful.
   The same is true of radiocarbon SPAs: every measurement has a
   calibration-curve uncertainty span across many years, but the
   field doesn't throw away the wide ones — they use Bayesian methods
   to combine partial information from each.
2. **Statistical power matters for the substantive questions.**
   √(180k) / √(30k) ≈ 2.4 × more power. Where you and the field are
   interested in **subtle** patterns — small Hanson β changes between
   provinces, decade-by-decade rate changes within a reign, regional
   differences in inscription-type composition — that 2.4 × factor
   decides whether you detect the effect or report a null result.
3. **Stratification breakdown blocks if you discard.** This is the
   big one. If you discard, then ask "what was inscription activity
   by province × type × half-century?", many cells have N < 10 in the
   discarded subset. The mixture model lets you ask that question
   with reasonable cell counts. The same goes for breakdowns by
   support material, urban / rural, language. Stratification depth is
   the difference between "the empire-wide pattern" and "the specific
   regional / chronological / typological mechanisms behind the
   pattern".
4. **Epitaphs in particular.** 56 % of the corpus is epitaph. In
   Cohort B (our best narrow-dated cohort) it's only 18 % — and the
   *absolute count* is 5,034. That's fine for an empire-wide aggregate
   but starves the per-province × per-half-century question. Funerary
   practice across the Roman empire is a major historical question;
   you cannot answer it on 5,034 records distributed across provinces
   and centuries.
5. **The mixture-model approach is a methodological contribution to
   the field.** Other epigraphic corpora face the same editorial-
   convention problem. SDAM (Heřmánková, Glomb, Kaše — the very
   people in the RAC-TRAC 2026 conference session) have flagged this
   as an unsolved problem. A validated mixture-model + empirical-Bayes
   calibration approach is publishable methodology *separate* from
   the substantive findings. That's two papers in one — the
   methodology paper and the substantive Hanson-scaling-and-decline
   paper.
6. **The narrow-dated subset is NOT actually a representative sample
   of the ancient activity pattern.** We showed this in the type-
   composition analysis: it's dramatically biased toward honorific,
   identification, military-diploma, and mile-/leaguestone
   inscriptions. To get a representative ancient-activity SPA from
   the narrow-dated subset alone, you must reweight by type. But
   reweighting by type with sparse cells (only 169 epitaphs in
   Cohort A; 5,034 in Cohort B) inflates the prior's variance in the
   very types that dominate the corpus. The mixture model, properly
   implemented, uses the *narrow-dated subset as a prior on p_gen*
   AND uses the *wide-dated subset to constrain α via the slab
   structure*. You get more out of the whole than out of either half
   alone.

## The radiocarbon-SPA analogy is the right one

This is how I'd frame the question for Martin and for an applied-
econometrician audience generally: **discarding wide-uncertainty
observations is *throwing away information*, which a Bayesian
framework is specifically designed to avoid**.

The radiocarbon community had exactly this debate twenty years ago.
Early SPA work (1990s) used cutoffs and threshold filters: throw
away dates with calibration-curve uncertainty > 200 years, then
sum the rest. Later work (Bronk Ramsey 2009; Bevan & Crema 2011;
Crema & Bevan 2021; Heaton 2022 carbondate) developed Bayesian
methods to use all measurements with their associated uncertainty.

The field consensus is now firmly on the "use all data with proper
uncertainty modelling" side. The reason isn't aesthetic; it's that
the throwing-away approach loses too much power for stratification,
regional analysis, and event detection.

The same argument applies here. An inscription dated "AD 101-200"
contributes 1/100 unit of probability mass to each year of the second
century. Across 13,356 such inscriptions, the contribution is 133.56
unit-years per year of the second century — not negligible. The
mixture model's job is to figure out how much of that is real signal
vs convention. Throwing them away loses the 133.56 / year baseline.

## Recommendation: do both, and let them triangulate

The productive answer isn't "discard vs mixture" but **"discard for
triangulation, mixture for primary"**:

1. **Run the discard analysis as a robustness check / supplementary
   analysis.** It gives an unbiased reference. Where the mixture
   model agrees, you have high confidence. Where they diverge, you
   have a diagnostic flag.
2. **Use the mixture model (with the empirical-Bayes calibration-
   cohort fix) as the primary analysis** — once we've validated the
   fix on the recovery grid.
3. **For specific scientific questions where discard is genuinely
   sufficient** (Hanson scaling on well-inscribed cities; specific
   dated events), **report discard as primary** and note that the
   mixture model gives consistent results. That is the most
   defensible framing for those questions.
4. **Frame the methodological work as a contribution to the field**:
   the family classifier, the empirical-Bayes calibration cohort, the
   type-reweighting — these are tools that the SDAM and EAGLE
   communities can adopt. Published once, applied to many corpora.

This positions the paper for two audiences simultaneously: substantive
Roman historians who want the Hanson-scaling result, and quantitative
epigraphers who want the methodological toolkit. It also gives Martin
something to push back on productively — he can interrogate the
mixture model's calibration cohort, and the discard analysis is right
there as a backstop if his interrogation reveals fatal problems.

## The honest constraint to flag

There IS a scenario where the discard alternative becomes the
responsible primary choice: **if the mixture model with the empirical-
Bayes fix STILL fails the recovery grid**. We do not yet know whether
the calibration-cohort approach breaks the identifiability ridge or
just shifts the bias slightly. If we implement it and the new recovery
grid still shows α-bias ≥ 0.05 across most of the α range, we are in
territory where the model's quantitative output is unreliable, and
the discard approach — with the structural caveat about restricted
scientific scope — becomes the responsible choice.

## Decision tree

A simple branching, given what we now know:

- **If empirical-Bayes calibration cohort passes the recovery grid →**
  primary analysis is mixture model + discard as supplementary
  triangulation. Methodology paper publishes the validated calibration-
  cohort approach.
- **If empirical-Bayes calibration cohort fails the recovery grid →**
  re-evaluate; possibly fall back to discard as primary, with the
  mixture-model approach as exploratory + methodological-discussion.
  Methodology paper still possible, but frames the calibration cohort
  as a *partial* fix and identifies what would be needed next.
- **If the mixture-model approach is too expensive to implement
  properly before publication →** discard as primary, mixture-model
  approach proposed as future work in the discussion. Hanson-scaling
  paper publishes on the discard analysis; methodology paper deferred
  or split off.

The 2026-05-25 Martin consultation is partly *about which branch of
this tree we're on*. His view on whether the calibration-cohort
approach is methodologically sound is the deciding input.

## Bottom line

Yes, it is worth trying to recover from the 2/3 editorial convention
— **IF** we can validate the recovery. The "if" is the work that's
now in flight (the empirical-Bayes calibration cohort approach
proposed for tomorrow's consultation).

The discard approach is:

- The responsible fallback if the recovery fails.
- The right supplementary analysis regardless.
- Sufficient for some scientific questions on its own.

For the paper's substantive headline result (Hanson scaling and the
imperial-decline narrative), the answer to "which approach do we
use?" will be visible after the empirical-Bayes calibration cohort is
validated on the recovery grid. For the methodological contribution
to the field — the family classifier, the cohort taxonomy, the
type-reweighting — that is publishable regardless, because the
diagnostic work IS the methodological contribution even if the
mixture model itself ends up needing further structural revision.

End of rationale.
